# Color Design Notes [Tsujita Kunio]
## Episode 200: About *Revolutionary Girl Utena* - Part 2

With November's arrival, several of my jobs for next year have been cleared for information release.

First is *Witch Craft Works*. Director is Mizushima Tsutomu. J.C.Staff production. My credit for this work is "Visual Coordinate." It's a title for my project that I've been developing since last year to tackle work creation one step beyond color design. I'll write about this here sometime. And color design is by Ishida Miyuki from *Dream Eater Merry* and *The Pet Girl of Sakurasou*.

Another is *Wake Up, Girls!* Director is Yamakan - Director Yamamoto Yutaka. Production is Tatsunoko + ordet. With Director Yamamoto this is the third work following *blossom* and *The Miyakawa Family's Hunger*. I'm participating as color design. *WUG* has both theatrical work opening at year-end starting with Sendai and TV series in operation. Both start airing from January.

And Director Sato Junichi's OVA *Critically Endangered Girl: Amazing Twins*. This also releases in January.

Working hard like that! Please look forward to it! Thank you!

Now then.

Following Ikuhara and Hasegawa, Kaneko, Nagahama, and Takahashi began entering BE-PAPAS studio, and gradually the *Revolutionary Girl Utena* site was getting organized.

As for me, I commuted to the studio about once every three weeks (?). In my case, participating in *Utena* didn't mean quitting Toei Animation, but rather doing Toei Animation work fully as before while doing *Utena*-related work on weekends or late at night and such. Basic home work. Gradually bringing compiled results step by step, attending meetings at such a pace.

My work was making character color images concrete. After grasping rough color images shown in meetings with Ikuhara and Hasegawa, and color restrictions arising from story and character settings, I'd concretely add colors to character settings.

In most animation character color creation, the process is: character design gets OK → clean up character settings → begin color work (create color samples with cel animation). But *Utena*'s approach was to not decide line art design first, but decide design including color.

While Hasegawa would draw based on Saito Chiho's character rough drafts, actually the story wasn't a solid final version yet either - it was still a period of subtle fluctuation. So character setting directions weren't fully solidified, and character settings were still at the rough stage.

So I'd add colors to settings at that stage, but how to do this work? I didn't want to start making color samples using cel paints from the beginning. As I wrote earlier, since *Utena* was home work for me, first doing cel coloring at home was super troublesome. Setting up cel paints, light boxes, drying racks - all that stuff would be a major undertaking.  

Moreover, if you start thinking with cel paints from the beginning, you get restricted by paint color numbers and types, potentially shackling your ideas.  

Furthermore, the problem of which manufacturer's paints to use. STAC or Taiyo Shikisai? Well, since it wasn't a Toei work, it would definitely be Taiyo Shikisai, and then I didn't have those paints on hand... and so on.

Now I'd start like "well, let's scan line art into computer..." but it was 1995. While personal computers were gradually becoming usable in video and image fields, casually importing images and adding colors was still far from general use. Anime production sites were still in the analog era of cel animation supremacy.

So what did I do?

There's an alcohol-based color pen called "Copic," and I used that to add colors to paper settings for image creation. First I'd copy character settings using slightly good quality copy paper. Then I'd keep painting with Copic in my imagined color schemes. Well, coloring! This was good! Since it's alcohol-based ink you can layer like transparent watercolor, layering according to shading to create strength and gradation, finishing to look close to printed anime cel paint samples. Actually I jumped on Copic as soon as it came out and saw it at art supply stores, using it considerably for thinking about colors in Toei works from around the year before.

For now I got all available Copic colors and kept adding colors vigorously. Since these were just drafts, I tried many color sketches of many types from one setting drawing. It's probably past the statute of limitations so I'll write it, but actually that "slightly good quality copy paper" was secretly copied at Toei Animation (ah, sorry (sweat)).

I'd take those colored settings to BE-PAPAS. We'd spread them on the studio reception set table for opinion exchange with everyone including Ikuhara and Hasegawa. Then using scissors right there, we'd cut up the settings I'd colored for collage. This hair color, this outfit, but maybe this color division is better for the outfit? Like that. Furthermore, while doing that, "Should we add lines here to enable color division?" or "Maybe there should be something as a focal point on the shoulder part" - we'd change design along with colors.

Hasegawa would create new rough drawings of ideas that emerged, I'd add colors to those again and bring them, everyone would give opinions again, and sometimes we'd return to the previous design (laugh). We repeated such exchanges many, many times. Among such back-and-forth, Tougo's hair mesh and the student council's asymmetrical uniform color divisions were decided.

We continued such exchanges from 1995 into 1996. During that time I participated in *Dragon Ball* theatrical versions, did *Sailor Moon* *S* and *SS* theatrical versions - as always busy with Toei work, and during those busy periods *Utena* work would stop. But *Utena*'s broadcast start was finally looking like spring 1997. Then decision work would have to proceed all at once.
