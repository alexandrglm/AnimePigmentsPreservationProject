# Anime "photo"-"shoot" Course to Better Understand Anime \[Yoichi Izui]

## Episode 5: Considering Effects (1) (エフェクトを考える（１）)

This series is also approaching the halfway point. This time I'll write about effects. This is probably the most interesting part for everyone reading this. First, let's start with what effects in photography actually are.

Effects in photography refer to work that adds special effects as screen elements to composited cels and backgrounds to improve realism and enhance dramatic effects. That's a bit stiff (-_-;). To put it simply, it's improving appearance by increasing screen density. There's everything: shower in bath scenes, steam that prevents seeing important parts, beams fired in battles and explosions, various magic and sparks when swords clash, car headlights and light streaming into screens, out-of-focus screens - all sorts of things. Some you might not even consciously notice seeing. Today's anime uses these effect expressions extremely skillfully and in large quantities.

This is my personal view, but effects can be largely classified into two types:

**1: Environmental Recreation**  
**2: Creative Direction**

"Environmental recreation" means recreating various phenomena seen in the real world. Specifically, this covers surrounding environments, natural phenomena, and optical phenomena. In contrast, "creative direction" means creating phenomena that don't exist in reality as expression. Light ray, magic, and cyberspace expressions fall into this category. Texture expression and symbolic expression can also be included here.

This time, let's first explain environmental recreation effects.

*(Note) Regarding terminology, since each production site has dialects, this column uses unique interpretations. Therefore, words used here may differ from actual production site terminology. Also note that these are terms limited to anime photography and may differ from original video terminology.*

Speaking of environmental recreation, first is natural phenomenon recreation. Rain, snow, water, flame expressions fall into this category. These expressions are currently often created with plugins that operate on After Effects. Specifically, we use plugins that simulate particle (パーティクル) movement to create various expressions. Commonly seen examples include rain and snow.

First decide the quantity and shape of particles to generate, set parameters for particle behavior, gravity, wind effects, etc., and create each movement. Since they're designed in 3D space, depth and overlap between particles can be expressed, and setting floor surfaces for accumulation is also possible. Using rain as an example, first set fall amount, angle, speed, etc. Furthermore, since screen appearance changes depending on whether the cut is wide-angle or telephoto, we adjust rain amount according to layout.

Particle shapes were initially often expressed as simple lines or points, but recently the shapes and textures of falling objects themselves are elaborated to be more realistic. Since you can attach drawings or animation to set particles, you can create falling cherry blossoms, fallen leaf expressions, and simplified flocks of birds in the sky. In *The Wind Rises*, they attached several patterns of repeat-animated grass animation to particles for distant grassland expression.

The expression of dust dancing in light bands streaming into dark rooms is commonly seen recently, and this can also be created with particles. By adjusting parameters, you can make lightning shapes, turn them into light points to express bullets fired from machine guns, or generate text along grids to create "Matrix"-like flowing text expressions. While the points themselves don't move, twinkling stars in night skies and point light sources in night scenes can also be created with particles.

Plugin particles can express water, flame, smoke, etc., but since texture has limitations, more realistic expressions require creation with 3DCG tools. Water surface expression is also basically within photography effects scope, but when moving objects are involved or wave movements are complex - when extremely delicate expression is required - 3DCG is sometimes introduced. This is decided in advance meetings according to cut content. Even in such 3D production cases, seasoning parts like reflected light are finally added in photography. When explosion expressions themselves are created in 3D, like smoke and flames involved with explosions, they're often created simultaneously in 3D.

Steam, fog, sand dust, clouds, etc. can be created with particles, but when movement is minimal and covers the entire area, they're often created using an effect called fractal noise (フラクタルノイズ). This creates random black-and-white mottled patterns and can add movement where patterns change along the time axis. Since it's highly versatile, it's often used for other texture expressions and random movement effects. Based on this, we adjust color, shape, transparency, etc.

Adding comprehensive texture to expressions explained so far is also effects work. Most frequently used is probably transmitted light (透過光) expression. At anime production sites, this is abbreviated as "T-ko" (T光) processing. For example, with lighting or flames, we use materials created with animation or particles and apply processing to make them actually appear to glow. In film photography era we actually photographed light, but currently we express this using After Effects layer composite modes (レイヤー合成モード). This function decides how layered layers composite with each other, and by layering you can brighten or darken composite targets and change color tones.

For example, with light bulbs, we extract only the parts we want to glow from the cel - here the bulb's glass part - with processing to make a "mask" (マスク). Next, to divide that mask into light core parts, bulb body, surrounding diffused light parts, etc., we copy it multiple times, set light colors for each, and apply different blur values. We composite and adjust multiple materials created this way by adding brightness (actually, finer adjustments are needed depending on background color tone and brightness, but I'll omit that here). Flames, sparks, beams, spacecraft exhaust ports, monitor glow, etc. follow basically the same workflow. Character eye highlights and extremely strong reflected light expressions are also similar processing.

Next, let's explain optical expressions. I also wrote about this in the *Hibike! Euphonium* feature in "Anime Style 007." This mainly handles expressions of phenomena occurring when light passes through lenses and light behavior in natural environments as effects. Regarding lens expressions, in the opening classification this would be considered creative direction scope, so let's save it for next time.

Currently, what's most frequently used as screen expression in any work is probably environmental light (環境光). This recreates light entering from key light sources according to environments set in scenes and cuts. Representative examples are outdoor sunlight, indoor lighting and external light - light reflected on screen. Monitor screen glare also falls into this. Basically, both backgrounds and cels are designed considering light source positions, so this reinforces that role. Using wide circular gradations and halation expression materials (ハレーション表現用の素材) created for each work, we composite by adding brightness on screen using composite modes. Depending on light sources like light through curtains, movement may be added instead of still images (such light effects are collectively called flare or halation).

A method for expressing strong light is lens flare with ghost (ゴースト付きのレンズフレア). Ghost refers to when extremely strong light sources exist within or near the screen, light that irregular-reflects inside lenses appears on screen as connected circular or polygonal reflected images. This is familiar as video expression. It's often created using plugins that recreate lens flare, but can also be created with hand-drawn materials or masks.

Also, for expressing less intense light, we often use techniques adding flare without ghost to light sources. Examples include distant street lamps and lighting, metal surface highlights, etc. These are also created with plugins, but parameter settings can create various shapes, so they're used not just for simple light sources but also beam weapons and magical attacks.

The standard light ray (光芒) is cross light (十字光). It's also used in gag expressions where someone gets punched far away and finally sparkles! with cross light appearing while rotating (this is a creative direction usage example).

As a recent trend, we often see lens flare stretched horizontally in straight lines. Perhaps because it gives a stylish impression, it's become frequently used in anime too. Director Shinbo's "Monogatari" series are impressive examples. In live action, it's frequently used in Director J.J. Abrams' works. This occurs when using special lenses called anamorphic lenses (アナモルフィックレンズ) used for shooting widescreen footage like cinemascope in live action - it doesn't occur with normal lenses. Since anamorphic lenses compress and record footage horizontally, the lens aperture shape becomes vertically elongated, and light diffraction phenomena passing through creates such flare shapes. Another characteristic is that background bokeh also becomes vertically elongated elliptical shapes. While some works are apparently produced with proper awareness of such characteristics, most examples usually only use the flare shape. Even in live action there are dedicated filters for producing horizontal flare, so lens characteristics and expression aren't necessarily consistent. Incidentally, similar to horizontal flare but different is "smear" - a phenomenon where vertical light streaks appear. This is an error-like electronic phenomenon occurring on image sensors capturing strong light. So it's separate from the above lens flare.

Different from flare, but in light-related expressions, point light sources appearing as circular bokeh in blurred backgrounds is also commonly seen expression. It's used for fantastical sparkling light expressions like distant city lights in night scenes, water surface reflections, and sunlight filtering through trees. This is expressed with blur plugins recreating lens blur (other methods can substitute). These light shapes include circular and polygonal ones, but when passing through actual lenses, they're influenced by lens aperture shapes. When software allows specifying aperture shapes, you can change to preferred shapes. Recent lenses have mostly circular apertures, while polygonal ones might be considered somewhat classical. However, anime selection depends on work tendencies and director preferences. If specified, heart-shaped or star-shaped bokeh light can be created (conversely, with real lenses, if you attach cut-out paper to lens surfaces, similar effects are achieved).

Additionally, recently we see expressions of irregular reflection from dirt and dust on lens surfaces, as introduced in "Anime Style 007." Since I haven't researched this, I don't know specific creation methods, but I think materials used as dirt and dust are created with lens blur or sometimes hand-drawn, with some effects layered over them.

On top of such light expressions, we sometimes apply light diffusion effects (光の拡散効果). This is used for expressions like strong light hitting targets or being in steam or haze. Basically, weak blur centers on bright screen parts, with brighter areas appearing hazier. Applied overall it creates moist atmospheric feeling and is often used for bath or morning expressions. It's also used in blazing midsummer or evening scenes to emphasize sunlight strength. Overdoing it makes entire screens whitish and deviates from original color design, so caution is needed.

The light expressions explained so far occur within lenses. In other words, considering positional relationships with cels during photography, all are placed on top of cels. Particularly lens flare, when considering live action, disappears when light sources are hidden, so it doesn't occur behind cels (light diffusion does occur). Also, lens flare appearing on the same screen becomes roughly the same shape depending on light source strength. Such things are natural since they appear through lens characteristics, but animation isn't bound by physical constraints, so placing flare behind obstructions or assigning different shaped flare to each screen light source is possible. When such expressions occur, it's results of prioritizing directional aims and visual appeal, or simple misunderstanding...

Actually, there are diverse expressions and techniques that can't be fully written here - explaining each one could fill an entire book. Here I've listed extremely limited but understandable, representative examples. Since techniques described this time are all used daily, paying slight attention when watching anime might lead to various discoveries.

Let's stop here this time. Next time I plan to continue touching on creative direction effects.
