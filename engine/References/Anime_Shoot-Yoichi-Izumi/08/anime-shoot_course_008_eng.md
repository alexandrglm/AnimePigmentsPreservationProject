# Anime "photo"-"shoot" Course to Better Understand Anime \[Yoichi Izui]

## Episode 8 (Final Episode): What is Animation "Photography"... (  アニメの「撮影」って…… )

Well, this series has finally reached its final episode. Over these seven episodes I've written various topics related to photography, and this time I'd like to take up supplementary points and detailed issues. It won't be a very flashy topic for a final episode, but photography is inherently that modest - please stay with me to the end.

In last episode's topic about coordination with the art department, I touched on composite materials. Currently, "composite" work has become very frequent. Since digitization, overall screen density has increased, so even things that could previously be handled with cel expression now require density, using composite materials from art departments even for moving parts. If screen size is small, some things can be handled with color direction (色指定), but due to timing when materials reach finishing and other factors, work is often done in photography as the final process. Composite work basically uses several transformation functions. For simple perspective changes, we use a method called corner pin that distorts screens based on the four corners. For curved transformations, we use screen-bending functions called mesh warp or Bézier warp.

![Frame examples](./satsuei008-1.png)

Corner pin can only do linear transformations, so it's used for flat objects like monitor screens and doors. For simple shapes, even objects with thickness can be expressed by separately compositing surfaces and sides.

Mesh warp can be treated as curved surfaces, so it's used for books, newspapers, bottles, clothing patterns, etc. Mesh density can be changed. However, since it bends by moving several intersection points, when these control points increase, work becomes difficult, and careless stretching easily creates unnatural movement, so finishing with as few intersection points as possible is more efficient.

Composite materials can basically only handle rectangular shapes, so they're basically created as viewed from the front. When mistakenly distorted shapes are submitted, they must be returned to rectangular form before compositing. For special cases that can't be handled rectangularly, we use external tools for transformation and compositing.

In *Howl's Moving Castle*, we used software called ElasticReality to composite onto animated cels for many cuts, just for the castle's leg parts, expressing texture. Massive compositing was also used in the latter half's castle collapse scenes. In all cases, every frame must be tracked and plotted, requiring enormous effort. For long, complex movements, work must be done carefully over time to avoid unnaturalness.

Recently, voices question how far photography should handle compositing. With effort, even rotating objects can be created, but since they originate from flat surfaces, expression has limits. When things become complex, rather than spending effort, we use 3D mapping.

Related to compositing, I'd like to address text information. Many composite materials contain text, but recent high-resolution development has improved readability. Therefore, various responses are now taken on work sites.

First, responsible directors prepare proper content, and motion graphics staff also prepare logic. Initially, completely fictional character designs are prepared and assigned to numbers and the 50-sound system for use - like Zentraedi characters that became commercial products. There's also the method of processing with blur and effects during photography to make text unreadable, but it can still be deciphered, requiring caution (fans' dedication is amazing). Public domain or completely random character strings pose no problems, but when using actual books, copyright consideration is naturally necessary. Recently, consideration for religion and political correctness is also demanded.

Regarding text topics, there's also the typeface issue. Due to increased compositing, typeface (font) variety has also increased. Naturally, commercially usable ones are used, but usage regulations differ by manufacturer, requiring caution. Recently, comprehensive contracts with manufacturers from the beginning have increased. In such cases, font manufacturer company names are credited in staff rolls under names like "font cooperation."

In effects topics, I addressed bokeh. In anime, there are other expressions using blur. It's called motion blur, expressing movement speed. While lens blur reproduces optical phenomena through lenses, this reproduces states captured on film or sensors. It's an expression where movement is too fast and becomes blurred on recording media, used frequently. Action scenes are easy to understand, but it's also used just before frame-out to express large movement near the camera. When frame-out occurs, images tend to remain in eyes, so this has a mitigating effect. It's sometimes used on fast-moving backgrounds for effects separate from depth expression.

Sometimes it's applied to entire screens, sometimes only masked to desired blur areas. Furthermore, there are plugins that detect forward-backward movement and automatically create blur matching movement. When directors specify it, it must be included, but as with everything, excess defeats purpose - overdoing it can hinder animators' showcase movement expression. Personally, I think frequent use is questionable.

Speaking of overdoing, background blur application and environmental light/flare effects on cel colors in screens are also troublesome. If blurring is premised from the beginning, understanding is easier, but it's often decided at the photography stage, and when most things end up blurred, it's honestly discouraging. However, there's also the problem that proper blur can't be achieved without properly drawn backgrounds - it's difficult. The same applies to color direction. Even with color direction matched to scenes and backgrounds, if backgrounds are blurred, impressions strictly change. Adding light on top makes cel colors appear significantly different. If final screen appearance consensus is properly obtained through meetings, such headaches would decrease...

Speaking of color topics, current animation production sites work almost entirely in 24-bit color. This corresponds to commonly called full color - 16.77 million colors. Each of the three primary colors RGB is assigned 8-bit 256 gradations, displaying 256 cubed = 16,777,216 colors (remaining 8 bits are for masks). After Effects and Photoshop represent this as 8-bit color and per-channel values. Cels and backgrounds are also created within this color count. However, during photography, calculations use even more - 16 bits each for RGB. This allows using 65,536 gradations per channel, but since After Effects and Photoshop process at 15 bits, it's actually 32,768 gradations. Calculating with wide gradations allows smooth expression of gradations and blur. In the era when only 8 bits were available, tone jump phenomena where brightness appeared to change rapidly and Mach band phenomena where gradation boundaries appeared emphasized due to optical illusion occurred from insufficient gradation, but these have been considerably alleviated recently. However, for TV, since final output is often as 8-bit images, special attention is needed for dark areas requiring gradation expression. Methods to reduce gradation insufficiency include adding noise or diffusion, but screens become slightly grainy textured. Depending on works, such effects are sometimes applied strongly aiming for film-like texture.

Regarding color, there are various rules and precautions. First, monitor color temperature must be set. This is the same as white balance in photography - color temperature determines what color tone feels "white." Color temperature is expressed in K (Kelvin) units, with higher values appearing bluish-white and lower values appearing reddish. When monitor color temperature changes, displayed colors change, making image data colors seem changed, but image data RGB values haven't changed - only monitor display has changed. Standard for computer monitors is 6500K, adjustable mechanically or through software. For TV works, since Japanese TV standard is 9300K - a considerably bluish-white value - we match that. Working with incorrect color temperature monitors means carefully created color tones won't be reproduced on TV. For copyright materials with paper bases, setting to printing standard 5000K - a considerably reddish value - produces correct color tone.

Speaking of color temperature, appearance changes considerably depending on everyone's home viewing environments. As written earlier, Japanese home TVs are adjusted to 9300K, but are naturally changeable. Separately, there are image quality adjustment modes, and most TVs sold are set to dynamic mode. This has strong contrast with flashy color tone correction, so appearance is crisp, but fine nuances and subtle color tones are lost. As I wrote at the series beginning, work sites use professional flat image quality monitors for confirmation, so for enjoying color tones and image quality close to originals, I recommend adjusting image quality. This might better convey production side intentions. Recently, TVs and recorders with dedicated anime modes exist, but seasoning varies by manufacturer, so trying various settings according to preferences might be good.

For color, color space also requires attention. Colors expressed in RGB on monitors have standards called color space regarding color ranges displayed on monitors. Currently, sRGB used on computers and Rec.709 matched to HDTV seem mainly used. If all work monitor settings could be matched to these standards, troubles could be prevented beforehand. Ideally, art, color direction, and photography - departments related to color - should unify, but some inexpensive monitors lack adjustment functions, making it realistically difficult.

There's also a color profile system for matching color differences seen through equipment with different settings and characteristics like scanners, monitors, and digital cameras with used color spaces. However, incorrect settings can change data colors themselves. These profiles can be embedded when scanning or creating images in Photoshop, but when opened on other computers with different profile settings, colors are converted to appear the same. When the profile system was introduced, several accidents occurred where colors changed without knowing these circumstances. With subtle differences, materials flow to work sites without notice. Of course, dialogs appear when opening asking whether to convert or ignore profiles, and advance environment settings can prevent mistakes.

Over these eight episodes I've written various photography-related topics - how was it? This was my first life experience writing this much text, moreover for others to read, so there were certainly many inadequate parts - please forgive that. Content was also compiled from my observation range and things heard from acquaintances' work, so points likely differ by work site and production. Work sites are living things with constantly changing circumstances, and new expressions are born daily. Even so, I'd be happy if this column helps readers understand works more deeply when viewing anime in the future.

Finally, I'd like to state my personal feelings about "what photography is." When interviewed by a certain doujinshi previously, I compared it to Monopoly. The meaning was continuously going round and round the same place while consuming resources, but the stopping squares are always completely different. I basically still think the same, but I've also come to think it's time for serious self-reflection. All materials are drawn or created by other people, but whether they integrate into better footage depends on none other than my own work. What can be done here, am I not escaping, not giving up, is there still something possible - always self-questioning time (though there isn't that much remaining time...). Sometimes unexpected discoveries are made in such moments. That might also be part of photography work's appeal.

Thank you for staying with me to the end.
