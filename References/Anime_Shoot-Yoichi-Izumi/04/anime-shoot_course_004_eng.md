# Anime "photo"-"shoot" Course to Better Understand Anime \[Yoichi Izui]

## Episode 4: Considering Camera Work

This time we'll talk about camera work. Camera work in animation is a directing technique that adds some kind of movement to the screen, creating changes in the screen by moving angles and positions. This allows animation, which is originally flat, to pseudo-express movement, depth, height, and such.

In the era of film shooting, this was realized by actually moving cameras or moving materials, but currently everything is done with data, so some ways of thinking have changed. However, depending on the software used, some can be set up the same way as film era using virtual cameras. Why we care about this is because the presence or absence of cameras completely changes the approach to movement. Camera presence or absence becomes the difference between moving the camera or moving materials when creating movement. For example, to make the screen appear to move right, you'd fix materials and move the camera left, but without assuming a camera you could just move materials right (actually, moving materials is simpler, so moving cameras is rare).

While we say camera work in one word, there are actually multiple methods and explaining everything is impossible, so this time let's introduce only basic, understandable ones. Representative anime camera work includes the following:

**Zoom In & Zoom Out (Z.I & Z.O)** - Changing lens focal distance to get closer or farther from the subject  
**Track Up & Track Back (T.U & T.B)** - Moving the entire camera closer to or away from the subject  
**Pan (PAN)** - Swinging camera direction left and right while keeping position fixed  
**Follow (Follow)** - Camera moves at the same speed as the subject  

Zoom and track are subject size changes, pan and follow are horizontal screen movement. Here, those who thought "Huh?" are quite sharp. There's no vertical movement among these. In live action there are other techniques like TILT where you swing the camera vertically, and crane up as vertical follow, but in animation's case, there's a custom of calling vertical cases vertical pan (縦パン), pan up (パンアップ), pan down (パンダウン), so the word tilt isn't much used. Naturally the appearance differs, so when needed for direction, whether it's vertical pan or follow is properly instructed in layouts.

Actually, the above movements are combined to design even more complex camera work at direction and rough animation stages. This part is quite different from live action and uniquely anime. In live action, even just two elements of panning while zooming would probably require several takes (motion control cameras aside).

Regarding zoom and track, anime rarely makes strict distinctions. Considering actual cameras, zoom changes lens focal distance without changing camera position, while track doesn't change focal distance but changes distance between subject and camera, so appearances naturally differ. In anime's case of flat expression, such differences don't emerge much, so they're mostly treated similarly in actual work. However, when wanting to express lens distortion or characteristics for direction, instructions are included, so we consider separate responses like devising layouts or adding distortion and displacement in post-processing.

For screen size changes, there are two approaches in actual photography work. One method scales materials up and down, another treats them as planes in 3D space within software and moves them in depth direction within that space.

After Effects used for photography treats cels and backgrounds as individual layers and creates screens by stacking them, allowing each layer to be individually treated as a 3D plane. Previously we often handled this with scaling, but currently 3D conversion seems more common. Skipping detailed explanation, the reasons are that moving in 3D produces changes closer to actual cameras, and control is easier when there's frame movement along with zoom. If you treat scaling and movement separately, when applying acceleration/deceleration you need to synchronize each acceleration/deceleration value or you get strange movement, requiring synchronization with "expressions" (such extended functions exist), but with 3D you only need to control position changes and don't have such problems.

We perform zoom and track processing with such methods, but changing scaling values for each material layer, or in 3D shifting placement positions, creates movement displacement between layers overall when moved. Using this multi-layered approach enables depth expression. For example, when doing T.U on characters, if background T.U is slower than foreground, distance feeling is born between character and background. Such expression was shot using special photography stands called multiplane cameras (マルチプレーンカメラ) with glass plates arranging materials in multiple layers in film era. Digital photography made this easily expressible and it became widely incorporated in direction.

In film photography era, when multiplane couldn't be used, materials shot separately were optically composited, but that was costly and time-consuming. Image quality degradation also occurred. In *Castle in the Sky*'s ending, the cut where only background does T.B as Pazu and others' kite leaves Laputa is optical composite. Those interested should check it out.

Multiplane cameras were often used in early Disney works. Image searches will show many photos, so look them up if interested. The photography stand capable of multiplane photography used by Ghibli is currently displayed in front of Imagica's first screening room. Industry people might have seen it.

Now let's talk about horizontal movement. Pan and follow differ in whether camera is fixed or moving. In live action the shooting methods are completely different, but in animation's case, since drawing pictures is basic, the work is the same. What differs is the direction aspect. To clarify, let's explain both differences with the same situation. As an example, consider a cut capturing two facing people from the side. I've prepared rough drawings here.

With people at screen's right and left edges, suppose the camera captures each speaking person with pan and follow respectively at their speaking timing. In pan's case, camera is placed between both people, and camera direction turns toward the speaking person at speaking timing. In follow's case, camera is arranged parallel to the lined-up people, and while keeping direction fixed, it moves to each person's side at speaking timing. Thus swinging camera toward shooting target direction is pan, moving toward target direction or moving at the same speed as target is follow.

Considering live action, you can imagine person perspective changing and differences in how both appear. In animation's case, since we don't often draw perspective changes of this degree, people appear exactly the same, and what changes greatly in appearance is background. In pan's case, background movement is also fast with large perspective changes. In contrast, follow cases change according to camera speed and distance to background, but movement isn't as fast as pan. Considering examples like space where background is infinitely distant, if swinging camera the background stars flow fast, but following would barely show movement (actually we move at reasonable speed to show movement for direction).

While both have clear differences like this, photography-wise both become the same work of drawing pictures, so it's hard to judge without camera shooting or live action experience.

Regarding usage distinction, when frame movement is relatively small or speed is slow, follow-like movement often gets pan specifications. Conversely, when shooting targets are clearly continuing to move, it becomes follow instruction. In follow cases, storyboards (絵コンテ) and layouts show camera movement direction and material drawing direction. Written like "Follow←" and "Pull→" so screen-visible movement direction and actual background-pulling work direction are opposite. This can be confirmed in commercially available storyboard books, so searching for them is good too.

Like zoom and track, pan and follow also use multi (マルチ) technique - multilayering cels and backgrounds and changing each pulling speed to express screen depth. Most understandable is scenery seen from train or car windows. Foreground structures are pulled fast, background scenery pulled slowly. The farther back, the slower the pull, with sky stopped or moved just slightly. Creating such speed differences enables depth expression despite being flat. Recently this is sometimes created in 3D, but with solid layout and design, flat planes are sufficiently effective. Riding trains and automatically thinking "this pull is about XX mm per frame..." is classic "photography staff syndrome."

As an aside, in film era, multiplane photography setups were called "gondola multi" (ゴンドラマルチ), and flat overlaid setups were called "密着マルチ" (contact multi). Contact multi remains today in names like "密着引き" (contact pull) and "マルチ引き" (multi pull), but the term gondola multi seems to have disappeared. Instead it's sometimes called D.T.B, digital track back, but these names aren't unified either.

Other camera work expressed by pulling materials includes "tsuke pan" (つけパン). There seem to be names like follow pan (フォローパン) and angle-matching pan (角合わせパン), but since they aren't unified, they can cause confusion during actual work. Basically, we call it that for movements that chase shooting targets on screen. Even simple horizontal movement cases become tsuke pan when movement width is large and character size and perspective changes are considered.

The name tsuke pan seems to be coined on-site during Director Miyazaki and others' Toei Animation era. While unclear who created it, they said "we created it." Since it chases targets in the screen - that is, attaches camera to targets - it's apparently "tsuke pan," but there seem to be various theories about origins. Think of it as a cut following star movement toward a planet. On large-format drawn backgrounds, matching the upper-left corner to specified scales, move frame from A to B (also called scale pan/目盛りパン). Cels within frames are basically drawn at moving frame size, but when background-cel relationships become detailed, they're sometimes drawn at background size. Elements like frame rotation and zoom combine with this basic movement. As I wrote earlier, actual work moves backgrounds to frame positions rather than moving frames - the reverse movement - so mental conversion is needed. When zoom and rotation are involved, you need to match scale positions at screen center, but when that necessity isn't conveyed to direction, corner-matched layouts get brought to photography, making work troublesome.

Whether pan or follow, material pulls always have instructions in layouts and timesheets (タイムシート) as explained previously. Instructions either specify movement width in frames or specify moving how many millimeters per frame. This millimeter specification is tricky - After Effects used for photography only uses pixel units, so mm specifications must be converted to pixels. Specifically, we calculate from material resolution information. Resolution is units expressed in dpi (dots per inch), representing how much pixel density an image has per inch. For example, 200dpi means 200 pixels displayed within one inch width density. Higher numbers mean higher definition images (recently we also use ppi [pixels per inch]).

Currently, most cases use expressions that do this automatically. By inputting parameters like resolution, movement amount per frame, angle, and magnification when changing size, it automatically moves the specified mm number and direction. Animation production-specific software sometimes has such functions built-in from the start, allowing units like millimeters and inches.

Previously there was a BG pull mm specification of 0.01mm per frame that surprised me. With this specification, even one second only moves 0.24mm, so movement is barely noticeable within the cut... As expected it became a retake, but with digital photography you can specify down to 1/100mm units, so such things happen.

Finally, I want to cover "camera shake" (カメラブレ) often used in recent works. This also has various names like image shake (画ブレ) and hand shake (手ブレ), but it expresses subtle screen shaking from so-called handheld cameras.

Expressing screen shaking with photography existed previously. For example, rolling expressing ship swaying and screen movement (画面動) expressing explosion or landing vibrations. In contrast, camera shake is used for direction reasons like heightening realism in anime cuts that originally couldn't have shaking, or expressing emotional wavering from character's subjective view.

Regarding live action works, previously it was impossible to handle large film cameras handheld, mainly shooting with tripods and various movement devices. But with stabilizer development that cameramen wear on their bodies and camera miniaturization, free movement within sets and camera work close to people became possible. Simultaneously, subtle camera shake began appearing directly on screen, and sometime anime worlds began incorporating camera shake frequently.

Since camera shake has extremely strong direction elements, poor application can make it feel fake and damage realism. It's that difficult work. For somewhat random shaking, expressions can add it to some degree, but cases with clearly defined viewlines like subjective cuts increase difficulty. Sometimes rough animation provides movement trajectory specifications in layouts, but without them photography staff add it with their own sense (when I couldn't do it well, Iso Mitsuo taught me tricks). While not flashy movement, it has major impact on viewer impression. Recently this adds focus-hunting movement and becomes even more advanced.

Let's stop here this time. What I could introduce was just the surface, but camera work has deep aspects and is work where various elements complexly intertwine. It could be called a demon gate for new photography staff. Not limited to photography but also applying to direction and rough animation - when material composition becomes complex, even with mental design, things don't actually work sometimes. Before strange specifications confuse the site, proper meetings are important.
